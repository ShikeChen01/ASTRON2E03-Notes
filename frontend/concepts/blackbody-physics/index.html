Blackbody Physics

A perfect absorber and emitter whose spectrum depends only on temperature — the foundation of stellar astrophysics.

Key equations

B_\nu(T) = \frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2} \cdot \frac{1}{e^{h\nu/kT}-1}

B_\lambda(T) = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5} \cdot \frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda kT}-1}

\lambda_\text{max} \cdot T = 2.898 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m·K}

F = \sigma T^4, \quad \sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\ \text{K}^{-4}

L = 4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4

Interactive demo

What you're seeing

The log-log plot shows the Planck spectral radiance B_\lambda(T) versus wavelength. The highlighted curve (purple) is the current temperature; faint grey curves show the Sun, Earth, and Jupiter for comparison. The dashed vertical line marks Wien's peak \lambda_\text{max} — it shifts left (bluer, shorter wavelength) as temperature rises and right (redder, longer) as it falls. The shaded band (0.38–0.75 µm) is the human visible range: hot stars peak there or in the UV, while planets peak deep in the infrared.

Try this

  • Drag the slider to ~3000 K (a red dwarf or incandescent bulb) and notice how little of the emission falls in the visible band — most energy is wasted as heat.
  • Click "Earth 288 K" and observe that the peak is around 10 µm (far-IR), which is why greenhouse gases absorbing at those wavelengths are so important for climate.
  • Compare Sun and Jupiter: the Sun's curve towers far above Jupiter's — even normalised, the peak is 40× hotter and the flux goes as T^4, so it is billions of times more luminous per unit area.
  • Push the slider to 10 000 K (a hot A-type star) and watch the peak move into the UV, explaining why such stars appear blue-white.
