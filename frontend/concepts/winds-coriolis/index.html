Winds: Coriolis, Hadley Cells, Zonal Bands

How planetary rotation sculpts atmospheric circulation — from Coriolis deflection to Hadley cells and Jupiter's striped jets.

Key equations

\vec{a}_c = -2\,\vec{\Omega} \times \vec{v}

Ro = \frac{U}{2\,\Omega\,L}

v = \frac{1}{2\,\rho\,\omega\sin\phi}\,\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}

Variables
\vec{a}_c
Coriolis acceleration (m s⁻²); deflects right in N hemisphere, left in S
\vec{\Omega}
planetary rotation vector, pointing along spin axis (rad s⁻¹)
\vec{v}
velocity of the air parcel in the rotating frame (m s⁻¹)
Ro
Rossby number — ratio of inertial to Coriolis forces; Earth ≈ 0.1, Jupiter ≈ 30
U
characteristic wind speed (m s⁻¹)
L
characteristic length scale of the flow (m)
v
geostrophic wind speed — pressure-balanced zonal flow (m s⁻¹)
\rho
air density (kg m⁻³)
\phi
latitude (rad); \sin\phi gives the local vertical component of \Omega
\partial P/\partial x
horizontal pressure gradient force per unit volume (Pa m⁻¹)

Interactive demo

What you're seeing

The left panel shows a top-down view of a rotating planet (pole at centre). An air parcel is launched from a fixed starting point and — in the inertial frame — would travel in a straight line. In the rotating frame the Coriolis acceleration -2\vec{\Omega}\times\vec{v} curves that path to the right in the Northern hemisphere (Ω pointing toward you) and to the left in the Southern hemisphere (Ω pointing away). The parcel traces a circle of radius v/(2\Omega) — the inertial oscillation circle. The Rossby number Ro tells you whether rotation dominates (Ro \ll 1, geostrophic regime) or inertia dominates (Ro \gg 1, free-flow regime).

The right panel shows how the number of Hadley-type circulation cells per hemisphere scales with rotation rate. A slow rotator like Venus (very low Ω) develops one giant overturning cell — warm air rises near the equator, flows poleward aloft, and sinks at the poles. At Earth's rotation rate the flow breaks into three cells per hemisphere: the tropical Hadley cell, the mid-latitude Ferrel cell, and the polar cell. A fast rotator like Jupiter hosts many narrow zonal bands: bright zones (rising air) alternate with dark belts (sinking air), separated by zonal jets that are Kelvin–Helmholtz unstable at their boundaries. The schematic updates automatically as you drag the Ω slider.

Try this

  • Click Venus (slow). Ω is very small — the Rossby number shoots above 1 ("inertia-dominated") and the right panel shows a single Hadley cell. The parcel barely curves.
  • Click Earth (medium). Ro drops below 1 ("rotation-dominated"), three cells appear on the right, and the parcel traces a tight inertial circle on the left.
  • Click Jupiter (fast). Ω is large, Ro → 0, many zonal bands fill the right panel, and the parcel spirals very tightly.
  • Toggle N / S hemisphere. Watch the curved trail flip handedness — rightward deflection in the north, leftward in the south.
  • Drag Ω and U independently. Notice that increasing U widens the inertial circle (radius ∝ U/Ω) while increasing Ω tightens it. The Ro readout captures both effects.
