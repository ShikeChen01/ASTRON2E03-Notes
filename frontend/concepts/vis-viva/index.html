Vis-Viva Equation & Kepler's Third Law

Orbital speed at any point on a Kepler orbit, and why the period depends only on the semi-major axis.

Key equations

v^2 = GM\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)

v_{\text{peri}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM\,(1+e)}{a(1-e)}}, \qquad v_{\text{apo}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM\,(1-e)}{a(1+e)}}

T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\,a^3

Interactive demo

What you're seeing

The blue arrow is the planet's velocity vector. Its length changes continuously — orbital speed depends on the current distance r from the focus, not on time. The vis-viva equation makes this explicit: at fixed semi-major axis a (which sets the total orbital energy), speed is highest at perihelion (smallest r) and lowest at aphelion (largest r).

The energy readout E = \tfrac{1}{2}v^2 - GM/r (in normalized units) stays constant throughout the orbit — this is conservation of mechanical energy, and it is exactly the principle from which vis-viva is derived.

Kepler's third law tells us T \propto a^{3/2}: the period grows with the semi-major axis alone, independent of eccentricity. Increase a with the slider and watch the planet slow down and take longer to complete each orbit — a distant planet crawls because its orbit is energetically shallower.

Try this

  • Hold a fixed; slide e from 0 to 0.85. At e=0 the speed is constant (circular orbit); by e=0.85 it swings wildly between perihelion and aphelion.
  • Set a = 80: the planet sprints around a tight orbit and the period is short. Set a = 250: visibly slower, longer period. That's T \propto a^{3/2} in action.
  • Pause near perihelion and note v. Then resume, pause near aphelion, and note v again. The ratio should be (1+e)/(1-e) — check it yourself!
