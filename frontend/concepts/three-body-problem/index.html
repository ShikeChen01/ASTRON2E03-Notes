Perturbations, Resonances, and the Three-Body Problem

Why the future of three gravitating bodies cannot be predicted by any formula — only by numerical integration, one tiny step at a time.

Key equations

\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_i = -\sum_{j\neq i} \frac{G m_j (\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j)}{|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|^3}

\text{No closed-form solution for general } N \geq 3

\text{Sensitive dependence: } |\delta\mathbf{r}(t)| \sim |\delta\mathbf{r}(0)| \, e^{\lambda t}

What you're seeing

Three bodies of equal mass interact under Newtonian gravity. Each body simultaneously pulls on — and is pulled by — both of the others. The equations of motion are straightforward to write down, but Poincaré proved in 1889 that no general closed-form solution exists: you cannot write a formula that tells you where body 1 will be at time t. The only way forward is numerical integration — advancing tiny time steps and recomputing the forces.

The trail fading behind each body illustrates sensitive dependence on initial conditions: increase the perturbation slider even a little and hit Reset. The early paths look nearly identical, then rapidly diverge — exponentially in time. This is the hallmark of deterministic chaos. The Lyapunov exponent \lambda measures how quickly nearby trajectories separate; even |\delta\mathbf{r}(0)| = 0.001 blows up to order-unity differences within a few crossing times.

Real planetary systems survive long because the planets are well-separated (making close-approach chaos rare) and many near-resonances are avoided or stabilized. Where mean-motion resonances do occur — like Neptune's 3:2 resonance with Pluto — careful analysis shows they can lock orbits into stable configurations rather than chaotic ones. But the generic three-body problem has no such guarantee.

Try this

  • Set perturbation to 0.000 and hit Reset. Let the simulation run for about 30 seconds. Note roughly where the three bodies are.
  • Set perturbation to 0.010 and hit Reset. The initial triangle looks almost the same — but after the same 30 seconds the bodies end up in a completely different configuration.
  • Try perturbation at 0.001, 0.005, 0.050. Watch how the divergence grows faster as the perturbation increases — the Lyapunov exponent is amplifying every difference exponentially.
  • Pause immediately after Reset at perturbation 0. The three bodies start at the vertices of a rotating equilateral triangle — a configuration that is briefly symmetric, then quickly unravels.
