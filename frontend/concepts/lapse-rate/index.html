Adiabatic Lapse Rate

Rising air does work against gravity, cools adiabatically, and sets the threshold between stable and convective atmospheres.

Key equations

\Gamma_d = -\frac{dT}{dz} = \frac{g}{c_p}

\Gamma_d(\text{Earth, dry}) \approx \frac{9.81}{1005} \approx 9.8\,\text{K\,km}^{-1}

\text{Stability: } \Gamma_{\rm env} < \Gamma_d \Rightarrow \text{STABLE}; \quad \Gamma_{\rm env} > \Gamma_d \Rightarrow \text{UNSTABLE (convective)}

Variables
\Gamma_d
dry adiabatic lapse rate — temperature decrease per unit altitude for a dry rising parcel (K km-1)
T
temperature of the air parcel (K)
z
altitude above the reference level (km)
g
surface gravity of the planet (m s-2); Earth: 9.81 m s-2, Jupiter: 23 m s-2
c_p
specific heat capacity at constant pressure of the atmosphere (J kg-1 K-1); dry air: 1005, H2: ~14 300, CO2: ~736–850
\Gamma_{\rm env}
environmental (actual) lapse rate — slope of the ambient temperature profile (K km-1)
\Gamma_{\rm moist}
moist adiabatic lapse rate — reduced lapse rate (~5–7 K km-1) due to latent heat released by condensation

Interactive demo

What you're seeing

The plot shows temperature (horizontal) vs. altitude (vertical, 0–20 km). The red dry adiabat is the temperature a dry rising parcel would have at each altitude — it cools at exactly \Gamma_d = g/c_p. The blue moist adiabat is shallower because condensation releases latent heat, partially counteracting the adiabatic cooling. The green dashed line is the environmental (background) temperature profile — what the surrounding atmosphere actually does.

Stability is determined by comparing the environmental lapse rate to the dry adiabat. When a parcel is displaced upward: if it ends up cooler than its surroundings (env slope is shallow, \Gamma_{\rm env} < \Gamma_d), it sinks back — the atmosphere is STABLE. If it ends up warmer than its surroundings (\Gamma_{\rm env} > \Gamma_d), it keeps rising — UNSTABLE, convective. The shaded region between the two profiles visualises which regime applies.

Changing the planet preset rescales g and c_p, directly shifting the dry adiabat slope. Jupiter's hydrogen atmosphere has an enormous c_p, making its dry adiabat surprisingly shallow (~1.8 K km-1).
