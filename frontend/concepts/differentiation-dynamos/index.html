Compositions, Differentiation, and Magnetic Fields

How planets separate into layers — and why some planets have powerful magnetic fields while others have none at all.

Key equations

\text{Dynamo: convection} + \text{conducting fluid} + \text{rotation}

H_2 \xrightarrow{P > 100\text{ GPa}} \text{metallic H (conducting)}

\text{Differentiation: } \rho_{\text{core}} > \rho_{\text{mantle}} > \rho_{\text{crust}}

Variables & terms
convection
buoyancy-driven fluid motion — hot, less-dense material rises; cool, denser material sinks. Must be vigorous enough to dominate over diffusion for a dynamo to operate.
conducting fluid
a liquid that carries electric currents. Terrestrial planets: molten iron alloy. Jupiter & Saturn: metallic hydrogen. Uranus & Neptune: ionic water + ammonia ("hot ice").
rotation
planetary spin. Coriolis forces organize convective motion into coherent columns that amplify the magnetic field.
H_2
molecular hydrogen — the dominant gas in giant-planet atmospheres at low pressure.
P
local pressure. At P \gtrsim 100 GPa (~1 Mbar), H2 dissociates into free protons and electrons — metallic hydrogen.
metallic H
degenerate state of hydrogen that behaves like a liquid metal; this is the dynamo fluid inside Jupiter and Saturn.
\rho_{\text{core}}
average density of the metallic core (kg m−3). Earth core: ~11 000 kg m−3.
\rho_{\text{mantle}}
average density of the silicate mantle (kg m−3). Earth: ~4 500 kg m−3.
\rho_{\text{crust}}
average density of the outer crust (kg m−3). Earth: ~2 700 kg m−3.
differentiation
gravitational sorting: dense material sinks to the center, lighter material floats up. Always yields \rho_{\text{core}} > \rho_{\text{mantle}} > \rho_{\text{crust}}.

What you're seeing

Terrestrial planets differentiate gravitationally during their hot early histories: dense iron-nickel sinks to form a core (often a solid inner core surrounded by a liquid outer core), silicate rock forms the mantle, and the lightest material rises to become a thin crust. The liquid outer core is the key ingredient — it is both electrically conducting and able to convect.

Giant planets work differently. Their interiors are dominated by hydrogen and helium, but pressure increases so rapidly with depth that beyond roughly 100 GPa hydrogen transitions into a metallic state — electrons are freed from their atoms, making the fluid an excellent conductor. This metallic-hydrogen layer plays the same role that liquid iron plays in Earth's core.

A dynamo needs all three ingredients simultaneously: a convecting fluid, electrical conductivity, and planetary rotation. Mars lost its dynamo early because its small core cooled and froze. Venus rotates so slowly that the Coriolis effect is too weak to organize the convection into field-generating flows. The Moon's iron fraction is simply too small. Uranus and Neptune host dynamos, but in shallow ionic-water layers rather than metallic hydrogen — producing weaker, more tilted fields.
