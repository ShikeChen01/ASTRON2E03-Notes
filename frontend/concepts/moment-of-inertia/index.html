Moment of Inertia & Gravitational Figure

How mass distribution inside a planet is encoded in a single dimensionless number — and how rotation flattens the body.

Key equations

I = \int r^2\,dm, \quad C/MR^2 \in [0,\, 0.4]

A \le B \le C \quad \text{(principal moments of inertia)}

J_2 = \frac{C - A}{Ma^2} \quad\text{(dimensionless oblateness parameter)}

MacCullagh's formula and surface gravity

For a slightly non-spherical body, the gravitational acceleration outside the body has a small correction proportional to (C - A). This is MacCullagh's formula:

g(r,\phi) \approx \frac{GM}{r^2} \;-\; \frac{3G(C-A)}{2r^4}\bigl(3\sin^2\phi - 1\bigr)

The first term is plain Newtonian gravity (as if the planet were a point mass). The second is the correction from oblateness: it falls off as 1/r^4 (faster than gravity itself), so it matters most close to the planet. The latitude factor (3\sin^2\phi - 1) is +2 at the poles and -1 at the equator — so a flattened planet pulls slightly harder at the poles than at the equator, purely from its shape.

On the rotating surface, you also subtract the centripetal contribution. Replacing (C-A) with J_2 M a^2 and adding the rotation term:

g(\phi) = \frac{GM}{r^2} - \frac{3GMa^2}{2r^4}\,J_2\bigl(3\sin^2\phi - 1\bigr) - \omega^2 r\cos^2\phi

The third term, -\omega^2 r\cos^2\phi, is the "centrifugal lightening": rotation eats some of gravity to keep you moving in a circle. Maximum at the equator (\cos^2 0 = 1), zero at the poles. On Earth, the combined oblateness + centrifugal effect makes you weigh about 0.5\% less at the equator than at the poles.

What are monopoles, dipoles, and quadrupoles?

These are the simplest possible "source patterns" for a field, ranked by how detailed they are. Any messy charge or mass distribution can be decomposed into a sum of them — far from the source, only the lowest non-zero term matters. Each one decays faster than the last:

  • Monopole — a point source. Field is radially symmetric. Falls off as 1/r^2.
  • Dipole — a + and a - separated by a small distance. Field has two lobes; falls off as 1/r^3.
  • Quadrupole — two dipoles back-to-back, or equivalently a ++/{-}{-} arrangement. Four lobes; falls off as 1/r^4.

The visualization below shows all three. The top row uses electric charges (where you can have both + and -) so you can see what each pattern looks like in its purest form. The bottom row shows the same idea for mass, where there is no "negative mass":

  • The gravitational monopole is just any body's total mass — always present.
  • The gravitational dipole is \int \mathbf{r}\,dm, which equals M \mathbf{R}_{cm}. If you put your origin at the center of mass, \mathbf{R}_{cm} = 0, so the dipole moment is exactly zero. Gravitational dipoles don't exist in any useful sense.
  • The gravitational quadrupole is the second moments of the mass distribution \int r_i r_j\,dm — and those are exactly the moments of inertia A, B, C. So the quadrupole is the first nontrivial correction to the monopole, and it's parameterized by (C-A) for a rotationally-symmetric body.

How does the gravity quadrupole "work" without repulsion?

Important subtlety: gravity has no -M, so the gravitational quadrupole is not a separate field with inward and outward arrows like the electric one above. It's a correction on top of the always-inward monopole.

To see the correction by itself, compare the real lumpy field to the field a point mass at the center would produce, and look at the difference. The two panels below show exactly that:

The left panel shows the real, total gravity field of an oblate body — every arrow points inward, just at slightly different lengths around the body. Total attraction is always inward; nothing is being pushed away.

The right panel shows the residual — the difference between the real field and an equivalent point-mass sphere's field. Now you can see the four-lobe pattern: residual arrows at the equator point inward (the bulge gives extra pull, more than a sphere would) and residual arrows at the poles point outward (less pull than a sphere would, because there's less mass overhead). Add this residual back to the always-inward monopole and you recover the left panel — with a subtle but real 4-lobe deviation.

Flattening of a fluid body

For a body that behaves as a fluid (planets are fluid on long timescales), hydrostatic equilibrium picks an oblate shape with flattening

f = \frac{a - c}{a} \;\approx\; \tfrac{3}{2}J_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\frac{a^3\omega^2}{GM}

The first term is the body's own gravitational non-sphericity; the second is the centrifugal contribution (the dimensionless quantity a^3\omega^2/(GM) is the ratio of centrifugal to gravitational acceleration at the equator). Saturn rotates fast and has low density, so this ratio is large — f_{\text{Saturn}} \approx 0.098, visible through any telescope. Earth: f \approx 0.0034; Jupiter: \approx 0.065.

The Darwin–Radau relation lets you invert this: measure f and J_2 from satellite tracking, and you can extract C/MR^2 — which then pins down the interior density profile. That's how Earth's 0.3308 was originally determined.

What you're seeing

The moment-of-inertia factor C/MR^2 is a single dimensionless number that captures how centrally concentrated a body's mass is. A perfectly uniform sphere gives exactly 2/5 = 0.400; any concentration of mass toward the center drives it below that value. Earth's measured value of 0.3308 was historically the first quantitative evidence for an iron core — long before seismology confirmed it.
