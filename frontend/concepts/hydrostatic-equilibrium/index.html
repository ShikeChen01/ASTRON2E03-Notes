Hydrostatic Equilibrium and Central Pressure

How a planet's interior pressure balances gravity — and how to estimate the pressure at the very center.

Key equations

\frac{dP}{dr} = -\rho(r)\,g(r), \qquad \frac{dM}{dr} = 4\pi r^2 \rho(r)

g(r) = \frac{4\pi G \rho r}{3}, \quad P(r) = \frac{2\pi G \rho^2}{3}(R^2 - r^2)

P_c \approx \frac{3GM^2}{8\pi R^4}

Variables
r
distance from the planet's center (0 \le r \le R)
R
planet's total radius (surface)
M
planet's total mass
M(r)
mass enclosed within radius r
\rho(r)
local density at radius r (uniform: \rho = M/V)
P(r)
pressure at radius r — what we're solving for
P_c
central pressure, P(0)
g(r)
gravitational acceleration at radius r (points inward)
G
Newton's gravitational constant, 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\,\text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2

What you're seeing

Hydrostatic equilibrium is the same balance you find in a stack of plates — each layer must support the weight of everything above it. Integrating this condition from the surface inward gives the pressure profile. For a uniform-density sphere the result is a parabola: pressure rises quadratically from zero at the surface to a peak at the center.

The left panel shows the planet cross-section colored by pressure — brightest yellow at the center, darkening through orange to deep blue at the surface. The right panel plots P(r)/P_c against r/R; it always hits 1 at the center and 0 at the surface regardless of planet size.

The central-pressure formula P_c = 3GM^2/(8\pi R^4) is an order-of-magnitude estimate. Real planets have denser cores, so true central pressures are typically 2–10× higher than the uniform-density prediction.

Try this

  • Click Earth: P_c \approx 1.7\,\text{Mbar} (uniform estimate), about half the model value of 3.6 Mbar.
  • Click Jupiter: P_c soars by orders of magnitude due to the M^2 scaling in the formula.
  • Try doubling R while holding M fixed: P_c drops by 16× (the 1/R^4 dependence), which is why low-density fluffy planets have small central pressures.
