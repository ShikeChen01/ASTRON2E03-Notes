Atmospheric Escape

Why some worlds keep their air and others lose it — from Jeans escape to hydrodynamic blowoff.

Key equations

v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2GM}{R}}

v_m = \sqrt{\dfrac{2kT}{m}}

f(v) = 4\pi\!\left(\frac{m}{2\pi kT}\right)^{\!3/2} v^2 \exp\!\left(-\frac{mv^2}{2kT}\right)

\text{Jeans criterion: escape significant when } v_\text{esc} \lesssim 3\,v_m

Variables
v_\text{esc}
escape velocity from the planetary surface (km s−1)
G
gravitational constant, 6.674 \times 10^{-11} N m² kg−2
M
planetary mass (kg)
R
planetary radius (m)
v_m
most probable thermal speed from Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution (km s−1)
k
Boltzmann constant, 1.381 \times 10^{-23} J K−1
T
temperature of the exosphere (K)
m
mass of one molecule (kg = atomic mass × 1.6605 \times 10^{-27} kg)
v
molecular speed (m s−1) — integration variable
f(v)
Maxwell–Boltzmann probability density (s m−1); \int f(v)\,dv = 1
3\,v_m
rule-of-thumb threshold — species with 3 v_m > v_\text{esc} escapes rapidly

Interactive demo

What you're seeing

The left panel shows the Maxwell–Boltzmann speed distribution f(v) for the selected species and temperature. The dashed vertical line marks the planet's escape velocity v_\text{esc}; the area under the curve to its right (shaded red) is the Jeans escape fraction — the fraction of molecules in the high-velocity tail that already exceed escape speed. A second dashed line marks the most probable speed v_m.

The right panel is a horizontal bar chart of v_\text{esc} for five solar-system bodies. The thin vertical rule shows 3\,v_m for the currently selected species and temperature. Bars are green (retains), red (escapes rapidly), or yellow (leaks slowly).

Jeans escape preferentially removes light isotopes, leaving heavier ones behind. The very high D/H ratio on Venus is a fingerprint of massive ancient water loss: hydrogen escaped faster than deuterium, enriching the residual atmosphere in the heavy isotope. Beyond Jeans escape, stellar XUV radiation can drive hydrodynamic escape (a bulk atmospheric wind), observed via Lyα and He 10830 Å transit absorption. Other loss mechanisms — photodissociation, charge exchange, polar wind, ion sputtering — become important for low-gravity bodies and are sensitive to stellar activity.

Try this

  • Select H₂ at 900 K. The readout should show 3·vm ≈ 11.8 km/s — just above Earth's vesc of 11.2 km/s. Watch the red tail grow and the verdict flip to "ESCAPES".
  • Switch planet to Jupiter. Even atomic H at 2000 K keeps 3·vm well below 59.5 km/s. Jupiter retains everything.
  • Try He on Mars (T = 300 K). Mars's thin atmosphere and low gravity make it vulnerable to helium loss — borne out by Mars's depleted noble-gas inventory.
  • Raise T to 2000 K and step through species from H to CO₂. Notice how quickly the tail fraction collapses as molecular mass increases — isotopic fractionation in action.
  • Compare Moon vs Mars for N₂. The Moon's vesc is so low that essentially all nitrogen escapes, explaining why the Moon has no stable atmosphere.
