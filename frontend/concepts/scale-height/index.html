Hydrostatic Atmosphere & Scale Height

How quickly pressure falls with altitude depends on T, gravity, and molecular mass.

Key equations

\frac{dP}{dz} = -\rho g, \quad P = \frac{\rho R T}{\mu_m}

P(z) = P_0\, e^{-z/H}, \quad H = \frac{kT}{mg} = \frac{R T}{\mu_m\, g}

\text{Column mass per m}^2 = \rho_0 H = \frac{P_0}{g}

Variables
P
atmospheric pressure (Pa)
\rho
atmospheric mass density (kg m−3)
z
altitude above reference level (m or km)
g
surface gravitational acceleration (m s−2)
R
universal gas constant, 8.314 J mol−1 K−1
T
temperature (K) — assumed constant (isothermal)
\mu_m
mean molar mass of the atmosphere (kg mol−1)
H
scale height — altitude over which pressure drops by factor e (km)
k
Boltzmann constant, 1.381 \times 10^{-23} J K−1
m
mean mass of one molecule (kg)
P_0
pressure at reference altitude z = 0 (Pa)
\rho_0
density at reference altitude z = 0 (kg m−3)

Interactive demo

What you're seeing

The left panel shows a semi-log plot of P(z)/P_0 = e^{-z/H} versus altitude in km. Because the y-axis is logarithmic the exponential decay becomes a straight line; the slope is steeper (faster pressure drop) for smaller scale heights. Faint grey reference curves show Earth, Venus, Mars, and Jupiter for comparison. Dashed horizontal lines mark multiples of the current H.

The right panel is a cartoon atmospheric column. The blue glow represents air density — bright and opaque at the base, fading to nothing over roughly 5 scale heights. A taller, more transparent column means a larger H.

Try this

  • Switch to Jupiter. Notice how H jumps to ~26 km despite Jupiter's strong gravity — its hydrogen atmosphere is so light that each molecule weighs far less, so the pressure gradient is gentler.
  • Drag T up to 1000 K while keeping Earth's g and μ. How does the column cartoon change? What does that imply about a heated exoplanet losing its atmosphere?
  • Compare Venus and Mars: similar \mu_m (CO₂-dominated), but very different T and g. Which effect dominates the scale height difference?
  • Read off P(10\,\text{km})/P_0 for Earth's preset (~0.26). This roughly matches real aviation altitude data.
