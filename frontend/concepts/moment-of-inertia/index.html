Moment of Inertia & Gravitational Figure

How mass distribution inside a planet is encoded in a single dimensionless number — and how rotation flattens the body.

Key equations

I = \int r^2\,dm, \quad C/MR^2 \in [0,\, 0.4]

J_2 = \frac{C - A}{Ma^2}

f = \frac{a - c}{a} \approx \tfrac{3}{2}J_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\frac{a^3\omega^2}{GM}

What you're seeing

The moment-of-inertia factor C/MR^2 is a single dimensionless number that captures how centrally concentrated a body's mass is. A perfectly uniform sphere gives exactly 2/5 = 0.400; any concentration of mass toward the center drives it below that value. Earth's measured value of 0.3308 was historically the first quantitative evidence for an iron core — long before seismology confirmed it.

The left panel shows a two-layer cross-section: a dense inner core (red, radius r_c = x R) surrounded by a less-dense mantle (blue). Drag the core fraction and density-contrast sliders to see how the computed C/MR^2 changes. The right panel displays the live value alongside Earth and Moon reference lines.

The small ellipse in the lower-right shows the equatorial bulge that rotation produces. For a fluid body in hydrostatic equilibrium, faster spin → larger flattening f = (a-c)/a. Saturn's f \approx 0.098 is visible to a telescope; Earth's f \approx 0.0034 is subtle but measurable from satellite orbits.

Try this

  • Set k = 1 (uniform): no matter what core fraction you choose, C/MR^2 = 0.400 exactly — the layers are identical so their ratio never changes.
  • Click Earth: C/MR^2 lands near 0.33, matching the observed value and confirming the large iron core.
