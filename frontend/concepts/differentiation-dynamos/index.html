What you're seeing

Terrestrial planets differentiate gravitationally during their hot early histories: dense iron-nickel sinks to form a core (often a solid inner core surrounded by a liquid outer core), silicate rock forms the mantle, and the lightest material rises to become a thin crust. The liquid outer core is the key ingredient — it is both electrically conducting and able to convect.

Giant planets work differently. Their interiors are dominated by hydrogen and helium, but pressure increases so rapidly with depth that beyond roughly 100 GPa hydrogen transitions into a metallic state — electrons are freed from their atoms, making the fluid an excellent conductor. This metallic-hydrogen layer plays the same role that liquid iron plays in Earth's core.

A dynamo needs all three ingredients simultaneously: a convecting fluid, electrical conductivity, and planetary rotation. Mars lost its dynamo early because its small core cooled and froze. Venus rotates so slowly that the Coriolis effect is too weak to organize the convection into field-generating flows. The Moon's iron fraction is simply too small. Uranus and Neptune host dynamos, but in shallow ionic-water layers rather than metallic hydrogen — producing weaker, more tilted fields.
