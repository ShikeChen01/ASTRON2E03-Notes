The Two-Body Problem

Two masses orbiting their common center of mass — and why it reduces to a single Kepler orbit.

Key equations

\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = -\frac{GM}{r^3}\mathbf{r}, \quad M = m_1 + m_2

\mathbf{R}_{cm} = \frac{m_1 \mathbf{r}_1 + m_2 \mathbf{r}_2}{M}

\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}

r_1 = \frac{m_2}{M} r, \qquad r_2 = \frac{m_1}{M} r

What you're seeing

Both bodies orbit the same center of mass (the small cross at the canvas center). The CoM never accelerates — it is the natural pivot of the system. Each body traces its own ellipse about that point, with the same eccentricity but radii inversely proportional to mass: the heavier body (m_1, \text{yellow}) stays close in, while the lighter body (m_2, \text{blue}) sweeps the larger orbit.

The full two-body dynamics collapse onto a single one-body problem: the relative coordinate \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1 obeys exactly Kepler's equation with total mass M, and the reduced mass \mu = m_1 m_2 / M encodes how much inertia the relative motion feels. This is why standard orbital mechanics textbooks treat the Sun as fixed — in the extreme mass-ratio limit, the Sun's ellipse shrinks to a point.

Try this

  • Set ratio 1:1: both bodies trace identical ellipses, always 180° apart. The CoM sits exactly at the midpoint of the segment joining them at every instant.
  • Set ratio 10:1: the heavy body barely moves; the light body sweeps the big orbit. This is the standard "fixed Sun" approximation used in introductory orbital mechanics.
  • Set ratio 5:1 with e = 0.6: both bodies trace clearly different ellipses, but their perihelion and aphelion timings align perfectly — watch the symmetry around the CoM as they accelerate together near closest approach.
