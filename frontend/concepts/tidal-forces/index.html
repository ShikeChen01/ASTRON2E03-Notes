Tidal Forces

Why gravity's gradient stretches bodies into ellipsoids — and how the 1/d³ falloff beats the Sun's raw mass.

Key equations

g(d) = \frac{GM}{d^2}

F_{\text{tide}} \approx \frac{2GMR}{d^3} \quad \text{(differential acceleration across radius } R\text{)}

F_{\text{tide}} \propto \frac{1}{d^3}

d_{\text{Roche}} \approx 1.26\, R_M \left(\frac{\rho_M}{\rho_m}\right)^{1/3}

What you're seeing

Gravity itself weakens as 1/d^2 — double your distance from a planet and the pull falls to one quarter. But tides are not caused by gravity directly; they arise from the difference in gravity across an extended body. The near side of a moon of radius R sits at d - R from the primary, the far side at d + R. That gradient scales as dg/dd \cdot R \propto 1/d^3 — a full extra power of distance in the denominator.

The result is stretching along the primary–secondary axis: one bulge points toward the primary (the near side is pulled harder) and one points away (the far side is pulled less, so it lags behind). Earth's oceans show both bulges simultaneously, which is why most coastal locations experience two high tides per day.

The perpendicular directions are squeezed inward: conservation of volume means that if the body stretches radially it must contract laterally. Watch the small inward arrows at the top and bottom of the secondary.

Roche limit: if the secondary comes close enough, tidal stretching exceeds its own self-gravity and it breaks apart. The rigid-body Roche limit is d_\text{Roche} \approx 1.26\,R_M(\rho_M/\rho_m)^{1/3}. In this demo the threshold is set at d = 100 px — drag the slider below that to see disruption.

Moon vs Sun: the Sun is ~27 million times more massive than the Moon, but the Moon is ~390× closer. Because tidal force scales as M/d^3, the Moon's tidal influence on Earth is roughly (390)^3 / (2.7 \times 10^7) \approx 2.2× stronger than the Sun's — the cubic falloff dominates.

Try this

  • Drag d from 700 to 200. The deformation grows dramatically. The 1/d³ readout changes by a factor of (700/200)^3 \approx 42.9, while the 1/d² readout only changes by (700/200)^2 \approx 12.2.
  • Bring d below 100: the body crosses its Roche limit. The ellipse turns red and a disruption warning appears on canvas.
  • Verify the 1/d³ law by checking two positions: at d = 400 the tidal readout is 1.00; at d = 200 it should read exactly 8.00 (half the distance → 2³ = 8× stronger).
  • Compare Moon–Sun: the Moon raises roughly 2.2× stronger tides than the Sun, despite the Sun being far more massive, because the Moon's proximity wins at 1/d³.
