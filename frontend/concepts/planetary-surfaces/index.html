Surfaces — Cratering, Volcanism, Tectonics, Erosion

Four geological processes compete to shape terrestrial planet surfaces. Crater density is a clock: old surfaces are heavily cratered, young ones are not.

Key equations

D_{\text{crater}} \approx 10 \cdot D_{\text{impactor}}

N(>D) \propto D^{-b}, \quad b \approx 2

\text{Crater density} \;\propto\; \text{surface age}

What you're seeing

The left panel shows a 100 km × 100 km patch of planetary crust accumulating impact craters over time. Crater diameters are drawn from a power-law distribution N(>D) \propto D^{-2} — small craters vastly outnumber large ones. The right panel displays live statistics and a surface-age estimate derived from crater density.

Crater density as a clock: craters accumulate at a roughly steady rate (after the early bombardment). A surface that has never been resurfaced preserves the full record — the Moon's far side records ~4 Gyr of impacts. Earth's surface is constantly refreshed by plate tectonics, volcanism, and erosion, so only ~190 confirmed impact structures survive. Toggle Volcanism to see craters get buried; toggle Erosion to watch rims gradually fade. Click Io to see the extreme: continuous volcanic resurfacing keeps the crater count at zero.

Tectonics adds a slow lateral shear to the surface pattern, representing how plate motion warps and disrupts the crust. In reality tectonics also drives mountain building, rifting, and subduction — all of which destroy crater records.

Try this

  • Click Moon: craters accumulate quickly and persist forever — the surface ages before your eyes.
  • Click Earth: craters appear but are steadily erased — the crater count reaches a low equilibrium.
  • Click Io: volcanism is so intense that craters never accumulate at all.
  • Click Reset, turn off all processes, and crank impact rate to max — watch the power-law size distribution emerge: many small craters, rare large ones.
