Equilibrium Temperature

How a planet's distance, albedo, and host-star luminosity set its expected surface temperature — absent any greenhouse effect.

Key equations

S = \frac{L_\star}{4\pi a^2}

T_\text{eq} = T_\star \sqrt{\frac{R_\star}{2a}}\,(1 - A_B)^{1/4}

Variables
S
stellar flux at distance a (W m−2)
L_\star
stellar luminosity (W)
a
orbital semi-major axis (m or AU)
T_\text{eq}
planet equilibrium temperature (K)
T_\star
stellar effective surface temperature (K)
R_\star
stellar radius (m)
A_B
Bond albedo (fraction of incident light reflected, 0–1)

Interactive demo

What you're seeing

The left panel shows a schematic of the star–planet system. The yellow circle is the star (scaled to R_\star); the dashed arc is the orbit at distance a; and the filled dot is the planet, colour-coded by T_\text{eq} (blue ≈ 100 K → white ≈ 273 K → orange ≈ 400 K → red > 500 K).

The right panel is a horizontal bar chart of T_\text{eq} for the five Solar System presets plus your current slider setting. Dashed reference lines mark 273 K (water ice point) and 373 K (water boiling point at 1 atm).

Earth's equilibrium temperature (~255 K) is ~33 K below its observed mean surface temperature of ~288 K — the greenhouse excess. Venus sits at ~232 K yet roasts at 735 K, the most dramatic runaway greenhouse in the Solar System.

Try this

  • Move the distance slider outward. Notice how T_\text{eq} falls as a^{-1/2} and the planet dot cools through blue.
  • Crank albedo to 0.9 (like a snow-covered world). How cold does Earth get?
  • Select Venus then slowly lower AB toward 0 — watch the equilibrium temperature climb toward what surface instruments actually measure.
  • Increase T_\star to 10 000 K (an A-type star). Where does Earth's orbit land on the temperature chart now?
  • Find the distance at which your custom planet sits right on the 273 K ice line.
