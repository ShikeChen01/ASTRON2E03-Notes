Cloud Formation & Phase Transitions

When rising air cools below the local saturation vapor pressure, gas condenses — forming cloud decks whose altitude and composition depend on the atmospheric temperature–pressure profile and the volatile species present.

Key equations

\frac{dP_{\rm sat}}{dT} = \frac{L \, P_{\rm sat}}{R_{\rm sp} \, T^2}

P_{\rm sat}(T) = P_{\rm ref} \exp\!\left[-\frac{L M}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_{\rm ref}}\right)\right]

\text{Cloud forms where} \quad P_{\rm vapor}(T) \geq P_{\rm sat}(T)

Variables
P_{\rm sat}(T)
saturation vapor pressure — the partial pressure at which a species is in equilibrium between gas and condensed phase (bar)
L
latent heat of condensation (or sublimation) per unit mass (J kg-1); e.g., H2O: 2.26 × 106, CO2: 5.73 × 105
M
molar mass of the condensing species (kg mol-1)
R
universal gas constant, 8.314 J mol-1 K-1
R_{\rm sp} = R/M
specific gas constant for the species (J kg-1 K-1)
T
temperature (K)
T_{\rm ref},\, P_{\rm ref}
reference temperature and saturation pressure used to anchor the Clausius–Clapeyron curve
P_{\rm vapor}
actual partial pressure of the volatile species in the atmosphere (bar)

What you're seeing

The diagram shows a log-log Temperature–Pressure plot. Each coloured curve is the Clausius–Clapeyron saturation curve for one volatile species: wherever the curve lies, a parcel of atmosphere at that (T, P) point is exactly at the condensation threshold. Above (higher pressure) and to the left (lower temperature) of a curve the species is in the condensed phase; below and to the right it is vapour.

The thick white diagonal line is the selected planet's atmospheric temperature–pressure profile, running from the surface (lower-right) up to the top of the troposphere (upper-left). A yellow star marks every crossing with a saturation curve — these are the cloud-deck altitudes. The readout panel on the right lists the inferred cloud species and their approximate deck temperature and pressure.

Different planets have very different profile slopes and volatile inventories, which is why cloud compositions vary so dramatically: Earth has H2O water clouds, Venus has sulphuric-acid droplets at ~60 km, Mars sports CO2 ice hazes, Jupiter has three layered cloud decks (NH3, NH4SH, H2O), and Titan has liquid-methane rain.

Try this

  • Click Jupiter: notice how the steep, cool profile crosses three saturation curves in succession, producing the NH3, NH4SH proxy, and H2O decks predicted by models.
  • Compare Mars and Earth: both have H2O curves, but on Mars the cold, low-pressure profile also crosses the CO2 saturation curve, explaining dry-ice polar caps and hazes.
  • Click Venus: the profile is so hot that only H2SO4 (which condenses at ~600 K / 1 bar) is crossed in the middle atmosphere, matching the observed cloud layer at 45–70 km.
  • Click Titan: the cold, dense profile passes through the CH4 saturation curve, consistent with methane rainclouds near 8 km altitude.
