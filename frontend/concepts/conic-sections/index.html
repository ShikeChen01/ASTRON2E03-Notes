Equation of the Orbit — Conic Sections

One polar equation produces every possible orbit shape: circle, ellipse, parabola, and hyperbola. Eccentricity is the single knob.

Key equations

r(\theta) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\theta}

e = 0 \,(\text{circle}), \quad 0 < e < 1 \,(\text{ellipse}), \quad e = 1 \,(\text{parabola}), \quad e > 1 \,(\text{hyperbola})

\text{Bound:}\ E < 0;\qquad \text{Marginal:}\ E = 0;\qquad \text{Unbound:}\ E > 0

What you're seeing

The single equation r(\theta) = p\,/\,(1 + e\cos\theta) — called the orbit equation in polar form — produces every possible conic section depending only on the eccentricity e. The focus (yellow dot) sits at the origin of the polar coordinate system, which is why it is the natural home for this equation: every orbit shape is described relative to the gravitating body.

Eccentricity is directly tied to total orbital energy E. A negative total energy (E < 0) means the object is gravitationally bound: e < 1 gives a closed ellipse (circle when e = 0). Exactly zero energy (E = 0) corresponds to e = 1, a parabola — the object barely escapes to infinity. Positive energy (E > 0) gives e > 1, a hyperbola, where the object swings around the focus and flies off to infinity on a straight asymptotic path.

The marker travels at constant arc-length speed to highlight the shape rather than Kepler dynamics. For hyperbolic orbits only the physical branch (nearest the focus) is drawn; the second branch would require a second, repulsive body.

Try this

  • Set e = 0: a perfect circle. The distance from the focus to any point on the curve is constant — it equals p.
  • Slowly slide e through 0.99 \to 1.00 \to 1.01: watch the closed orbit pop open into a parabola, then into a hyperbola. That transition is a change of sign in total energy.
  • Set e = 1.5: a wide hyperbola — this is the geometry of a gravitational flyby, such as Voyager past Jupiter. The dashed lines show the asymptotes the spacecraft approaches at large distance.
