Planetary Heat Budget and Cooling

Why small worlds go cold: how planetary size controls cooling timescale, and what heat sources keep larger bodies geologically alive today.

Key equations

\tau_{\text{cool}} \propto \frac{V}{A} = \frac{R}{3} \quad \text{(scaling)}

E_g = \tfrac{3}{5}\frac{GM^2}{R}, \quad \dot{E}_g = -\tfrac{3}{5}\frac{GM^2}{R^2}\dot{R}

\text{Sources: accretion} + \text{differentiation} + \text{radiogenic} + \text{tidal}

Variables & terms
\tau_{\text{cool}}
characteristic cooling timescale of a body (s or Gyr). Big bodies cool slowly; small ones cool fast.
V
volume of the body — sets how much heat it stores, V \propto R^3.
A
surface area — sets how fast heat radiates away, A \propto R^2.
V/A = R/3
the surface-to-volume ratio for a sphere. Larger R → more heat per unit radiating area → longer cooling time.
R
radius of the planet / moon / asteroid (m or km).
E_g
gravitational binding energy of a self-gravitating sphere (J) — the energy released during accretion.
G
Newton's gravitational constant, 6.674 \times 10^{-11} N m² kg−2.
M
total mass of the body (kg).
\dot{E}_g
rate of change of E_g as the planet contracts. Jupiter and Saturn still emit significant luminosity from slow Kelvin–Helmholtz contraction.
\dot{R}
rate of change of radius during contraction (m s−1).
accretion heat
gravitational PE released as planetesimals fall in during formation — the dominant early heat source.
differentiation heat
PE released when dense iron sinks to the core through the silicate mantle — a one-time pulse.
radiogenic heat
slow decay of long-lived isotopes (238U, 235U, 232Th, 40K) — dominates Earth's heat budget today.
tidal heating
mechanical work done by tidal flexing, e.g. Io from Jupiter — dominates moons with eccentric orbits.

What you're seeing

Left — cross-section: the body's interior temperature is shown by colour. At formation it is uniformly hot (\#ffd166 orange-yellow). As time passes heat escapes through the surface and the cool outer shell grows inward. The rate at which the cold front advances depends on \tau_{\text{cool}} \propto R — smaller bodies finish cooling in a fraction of the solar system's lifetime.

Small worlds cool fast because their volume-to-surface-area ratio is small: there is less heat stored per unit of radiating surface. The Moon and Mercury have been geologically inactive for billions of years. Mars still shows traces of recent (Amazonian) volcanism but its interior is largely frozen. Earth and Venus retain enough heat for ongoing mantle convection, plate tectonics, and — on Earth — a liquid-iron outer core that sustains the magnetic field.

Right — timescale chart: cooling timescales for five Solar System bodies on a log scale. The vertical line marks 4.6 Gyr, the current age of the Solar System. Bodies whose bar extends past that line are still geologically active (green); those that fall short are now cold and dead (grey). Gas giants such as Jupiter have an additional heat source — gravitational contraction — described by E_g = \tfrac{3}{5}GM^2/R, which releases energy as the planet slowly shrinks; Jupiter currently radiates ~3.5×1017 W more than it receives from the Sun.
